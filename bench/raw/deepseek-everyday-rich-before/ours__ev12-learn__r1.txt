# How Photosynthesis Works

Photosynthesis is the process by which green plants, algae, and some bacteria convert light energy into chemical energy, producing glucose and oxygen from carbon dioxide and water. It's the foundation of nearly all life on Earth.

<Card title="The Big Picture">
<Columns>
<div>

**The overall equation:**

6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

In words: **Carbon dioxide + Water + Sunlight → Glucose + Oxygen**

</div>
<div>

**Why it matters:**

- Produces the oxygen we breathe
- Creates the food (glucose) that fuels plants and, indirectly, all animals
- Removes CO₂ from the atmosphere, regulating climate

</div>
</Columns>
</Card>

<Card title="The Two Main Stages">
Photosynthesis happens in two linked stages, both inside the chloroplasts of plant cells.

<Columns>
<Card title="1. Light-Dependent Reactions" subtitle="In the thylakoid membranes">
- **Input:** Light, water (H₂O)
- **Output:** ATP, NADPH, oxygen (O₂)
- **What happens:** Light energy splits water molecules (photolysis), releasing oxygen. The energy is captured as ATP and NADPH.
- **Where:** Thylakoid discs inside chloroplasts
</Card>

<Card title="2. Light-Independent Reactions (Calvin Cycle)" subtitle="In the stroma">
- **Input:** CO₂, ATP, NADPH (from stage 1)
- **Output:** Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆), ADP, NADP⁺
- **What happens:** Carbon dioxide is fixed into organic molecules using the energy from ATP and NADPH.
- **Where:** Stroma (the fluid around the thylakoids)
</Card>
</Columns>

<Callout tone="info" title="Key analogy">
Think of photosynthesis like a factory: the light reactions are the **power plant** (generating energy), and the Calvin cycle is the **assembly line** (building glucose).
</Callout>
</Card>

<Card title="Step-by-Step Walkthrough">
<Steps>
<Step title="Light absorption" status="done">
Chlorophyll (the green pigment) in the thylakoid membranes absorbs light, mainly red and blue wavelengths. Green light is reflected, which is why leaves look green.
</Step>

<Step title="Water splitting" status="done">
The absorbed energy splits water molecules (H₂O) into oxygen, protons (H⁺), and electrons. The oxygen is released as a waste product — this is the O₂ we breathe.
</Step>

<Step title="Energy capture" status="done">
The electrons travel along the electron transport chain, powering the creation of ATP (cellular energy currency) and NADPH (an electron carrier).
</Step>

<Step title="Carbon fixation" status="done">
In the Calvin cycle, CO₂ from the air is attached to a 5-carbon molecule (RuBP) by the enzyme RuBisCO, forming a 6-carbon compound that immediately splits into two 3-carbon molecules (3-PGA).
</Step>

<Step title="Reduction" status="done">
Using ATP and NADPH from the light reactions, the 3-PGA molecules are converted into G3P (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate), a sugar.
</Step>

<Step title="Glucose synthesis" status="done">
Most G3P molecules are recycled to regenerate RuBP, but some combine to form glucose and other carbohydrates. Six turns of the cycle produce one glucose molecule.
</Step>
</Steps>
</Card>

<Card title="Key Factors & Limitations">
<Columns>
<Card title="What helps photosynthesis">
- **Light intensity:** More light (up to a point) speeds up the light reactions
- **CO₂ concentration:** Higher CO₂ levels increase the Calvin cycle rate
- **Optimal temperature:** Most plants work best between 65–85°F (18–30°C)
- **Water availability:** Needed for the light reactions and to keep stomata open
</Card>

<Card title="What limits it">
- **Too little light:** Shaded leaves photosynthesize slowly
- **Too much light:** Can damage chlorophyll (photoinhibition)
- **Extreme heat:** Denatures enzymes like RuBisCO
- **Drought:** Stomata close to conserve water, blocking CO₂ intake
</Card>
</Columns>
</Card>

<Card title="Common Misconceptions">
<Details title="Plants only photosynthesize during the day">
Actually, the light reactions need light, but the Calvin cycle can run in darkness as long as ATP and NADPH are available. In practice, plants do most photosynthesis during daylight hours, but some desert plants (CAM plants) open their stomata at night to store CO₂.
</Details>

<Details title="Photosynthesis and respiration are opposites">
They're complementary but not exact opposites. Photosynthesis builds glucose and releases oxygen; respiration breaks down glucose and uses oxygen. But they occur in different organelles (chloroplasts vs. mitochondria) and have different intermediate steps.
</Details>

<Details title="Plants don't need oxygen">
Plants do respire! They use oxygen to break down the glucose they produce, especially at night when photosynthesis stops. They're net oxygen producers overall, but they consume some oxygen too.
</Details>
</Card>
