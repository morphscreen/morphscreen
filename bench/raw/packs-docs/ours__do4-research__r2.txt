<Card title="Does coffee affect sleep?">
## Yes, coffee significantly affects sleep

Caffeine is a stimulant that blocks adenosine receptors in the brain, reducing sleep pressure and delaying sleep onset. Research consistently shows that coffee consumption—especially within 6 hours of bedtime—reduces total sleep time, increases sleep latency, decreases sleep efficiency, and reduces deep (slow-wave) sleep. The effects are dose-dependent and vary by individual caffeine metabolism.
</Card>

<Card title="Sources">
<Citation title="Drake et al., 2013 – Caffeine effects on sleep taken 0, 3, or 6 hours before bed" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24235903/" source="Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine" date="2013-11-15">
Even caffeine consumed 6 hours before bedtime significantly reduced total sleep time by ~1 hour and increased wake time during sleep.
</Citation>

<Citation title="Gardiner et al., 2023 – Systematic review of caffeine's impact on sleep architecture" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36725432/" source="Sleep Medicine Reviews" date="2023-02-01">
Caffeine consistently reduces slow-wave sleep (deep sleep) by 15–20% and increases stage 1 light sleep, with effects persisting even when consumed in the morning for slow metabolizers.
</Citation>

<Citation title="Institute of Medicine (US) – Caffeine for the Sustainment of Mental Task Performance" href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK221790/" source="National Academies Press" date="2001">
Caffeine doses of 200–400 mg (2–4 cups coffee) improve alertness but disrupt subsequent sleep; individual sensitivity varies 40-fold due to CYP1A2 genetics.
</Citation>
</Card>

<Card title="Confidence assessment">
<Stat label="Confidence" value="92%" change="High consensus across RCTs, meta-analyses, and mechanistic studies" tone="success" />
<Callout tone="info" title="Why not 100%?">
Individual variation in caffeine metabolism (CYP1A2 genotype), habitual tolerance, age, and timing of consumption create meaningful heterogeneity in effect size. Most studies test acute dosing in non-habitual users; long-term adaptation effects are less studied.
</Callout>
</Card>
