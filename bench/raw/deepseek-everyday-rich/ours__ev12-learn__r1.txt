# Photosynthesis: How Plants Turn Light into Life

Photosynthesis is the process plants, algae, and some bacteria use to convert sunlight, water, and carbon dioxide into glucose (food) and oxygen. It's the foundation of almost all life on Earth — it produces the oxygen we breathe and the food we eat.

<Card title="The Big Picture">
The overall reaction can be written as:

<Math tex="6CO_2 + 6H_2O + light \rightarrow C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2" label="Photosynthesis equation" />

In words: **carbon dioxide + water + sunlight → glucose + oxygen**

The glucose stores chemical energy that the plant uses for growth, and the oxygen is released into the air.
</Card>

## The Two Main Stages

Photosynthesis happens in two linked stages, both inside the chloroplast — the green organelle in plant cells.

<Card title="Stage 1: Light-Dependent Reactions">
These occur in the **thylakoid membranes** of the chloroplast, where chlorophyll (the green pigment) absorbs light.

1. **Light absorption**: Chlorophyll captures photons (light energy).
2. **Water splitting**: Light energy splits water (H₂O) into oxygen, protons, and electrons. Oxygen is released as a waste product.
3. **Energy production**: The energy is used to make two energy-carrying molecules: **ATP** and **NADPH**.

<Callout tone="info" title="Key point">This stage is called "light-dependent" because it needs light to happen. It's where the oxygen we breathe comes from.</Callout>
</Card>

<Card title="Stage 2: The Calvin Cycle (Light-Independent Reactions)">
These occur in the **stroma**, the fluid around the thylakoids. This stage doesn't need light directly — it uses the ATP and NADPH from Stage 1.

1. **Carbon fixation**: CO₂ from the air is "fixed" (attached) to a 5-carbon molecule, forming a 6-carbon compound that immediately splits into two 3-carbon molecules.
2. **Reduction**: ATP and NADPH from Stage 1 supply energy and electrons to convert those 3-carbon molecules into G3P (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate).
3. **Regeneration**: Most G3P molecules are used to regenerate the original 5-carbon molecule, keeping the cycle going. The rest is used to make glucose and other sugars.

<Callout tone="success" title="Key point">The Calvin Cycle is often called the "dark reactions," but that's misleading — it doesn't need darkness, it just doesn't need light directly. It runs during the day too, as long as ATP and NADPH are available.</Callout>
</Card>

<Card title="The Whole Process at a Glance">
<Steps>
<Step title="Light hits the leaf" status="done">Chlorophyll in the thylakoid membranes absorbs photons.</Step>
<Step title="Water is split" status="done">Light energy breaks H₂O into O₂ (released), H⁺, and electrons.</Step>
<Step title="ATP and NADPH are made" status="done">Energy from light is stored in these two molecules.</Step>
<Step title="CO₂ enters the leaf" status="done">Through stomata (tiny pores) on the leaf surface.</Step>
<Step title="Calvin Cycle runs" status="done">CO₂ is fixed and, using ATP and NADPH, turned into G3P and then glucose.</Step>
<Step title="Glucose powers the plant" status="done">Used for energy, growth, and stored as starch.</Step>
</Steps>
</Card>

<Card title="Where It All Happens">

|---|---|

| **Stomata** | Pores that let CO₂ in and O₂ out |
| **Chloroplast** | Organelle where photosynthesis occurs |
| **Thylakoid** | Stacked membranes where light reactions happen |

<Card title="What Affects Photosynthesis Rate?">
| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Light intensity** | More light = faster, up to a saturation point |
| **CO₂ concentration** | More CO₂ = faster, up to a point |
| **Temperature** | Too cold or too hot (above ~40°C) slows or stops enzymes |
| **Water availability** | Lack of water closes stomata, limiting CO₂ entry |
</Card>

<Card title="Common Misconceptions">
- **"Plants only photosynthesize during the day"** — True, but they also respire (use glucose for energy) at night, like animals do.
- **"The oxygen comes from CO₂"** — No! It comes from splitting water. CO₂'s oxygen ends up in glucose.
- **"Photosynthesis and respiration are opposites"** — They're complementary: photosynthesis stores energy, respiration releases it.
</Card>

<Card title="Try It Yourself">
If you have a plant, you can observe photosynthesis in action:

1. Place a submerged aquatic plant (like *Elodea*) under a bright light.
2. Count the bubbles it releases per minute — those are oxygen bubbles from the light reactions.
3. Move the light closer or farther away and watch the bubble rate change.

<Callout tone="warning" title="Safety">Always use a cool LED light or keep a gap so you don't overheat the water.</Callout>
</Card>

<Callout tone="info" title="Summary">Photosynthesis is a two-stage process: light reactions capture solar energy to make ATP and NADPH, splitting water and releasing oxygen; then the Calvin Cycle uses that energy to fix CO₂ into glucose. It's the ultimate source of food, oxygen, and fossil fuels, and it's happening right now in every green leaf around you.</Callout>
