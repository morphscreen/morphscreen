# How Photosynthesis Works

Photosynthesis is the process plants, algae, and some bacteria use to turn sunlight, water, and carbon dioxide into food (glucose) and oxygen. It's the foundation of nearly all life on Earth.

![Diagram of photosynthesis showing sunlight, water and CO2 entering a leaf, producing glucose and oxygen](find)

<Card title="The Big Picture">
Photosynthesis happens in two main stages inside the **chloroplasts** of plant cells (the green parts). The overall chemical equation is:

<Math tex="6CO_2 + 6H_2O + light \rightarrow C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2" label="Photosynthesis equation" />

**In words:** Carbon dioxide + water + light energy → glucose (sugar) + oxygen
</Card>

<Card title="The Two Stages">
<Columns>
<Card title="1. Light-Dependent Reactions" subtitle="Thylakoid membranes">
- Happens **only in sunlight**
- Chlorophyll absorbs light, splitting water molecules (**photolysis**)
- Releases **oxygen** as a waste product
- Produces energy carriers: **ATP** and **NADPH**
</Card>
<Card title="2. Light-Independent Reactions (Calvin Cycle)" subtitle="Stroma">
- Happens in the **stroma** (fluid around thylakoids) — can occur in dark
- Uses ATP and NADPH from stage 1
- Fixes CO₂ from the air into **glucose** (C₆H₁₂O₆)
- Regenerates the starting molecule (RuBP) to keep cycling
</Card>
</Columns>
</Card>

<Card title="Step-by-Step Walkthrough">
<Steps>
<Step title="Light hits the leaf" status="done" subtitle="Absorption">
Chlorophyll pigments in the thylakoid membranes absorb photons of light, exciting electrons to higher energy levels.
</Step>
<Step title="Water splits (photolysis)" status="done" subtitle="Oxygen released">
Enzymes split H₂O into H⁺, e⁻, and O₂. The oxygen diffuses out through stomata — that's the O₂ we breathe.
</Step>
<Step title="Electron transport chain" status="done" subtitle="ATP & NADPH made">
Excited electrons pass through protein complexes, pumping H⁺ ions to build a gradient. This drives ATP synthase to make **ATP**, and electrons reduce NADP⁺ to **NADPH**.
</Step>
<Step title="CO₂ fixation (Calvin cycle)" status="current" subtitle="Sugar built">
In the stroma, the enzyme RuBisCO attaches CO₂ to RuBP (a 5-carbon molecule), forming a 6-carbon compound that splits into two 3-carbon G3P molecules. These are used to build glucose and other sugars.
</Step>
<Step title="Glucose stored or used" status="todo" subtitle="Outputs">
Glucose fuels cellular respiration, or is stored as starch, sucrose, or cellulose for growth and energy later.
</Step>
</Steps>
</Card>

<Card title="What Goes In, What Comes Out">
<KeyValue title="Inputs & Outputs">
| Component | Role |
|---|---|
| Sunlight | Energy source (photons) |
| Water (H₂O) | Electron donor; source of O₂ |
| Carbon dioxide (CO₂) | Carbon source for glucose |
| Chlorophyll | Pigment that captures light |
| **Outputs** | **Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) + Oxygen (O₂)** |
</KeyValue>
</Card>

<Card title="Factors That Affect Photosynthesis Rate">
<Columns>
<Card title="Light intensity">
<Progress value="70" max="100" label="Optimal range" tone="success" />
Higher light boosts rate until saturation; too much can damage chlorophyll.
</Card>
<Card title="CO₂ concentration">
<Progress value="60" max="100" label="Optimal range" tone="info" />
More CO₂ (up to a point) speeds up the Calvin cycle.
</Card>
<Card title="Temperature">
<Progress value="75" max="100" label="Optimal range" tone="warning" />
Enzymes work best around 25–35°C; extreme heat denatures RuBisCO.
</Card>
<Card title="Water availability">
<Progress value="80" max="100" label="Optimal range" tone="success" />
Water stress closes stomata, cutting CO₂ intake.
</Card>
</Columns>
</Card>

<Card title="Why It Matters">
<Callout tone="success" title="The oxygen we breathe">
Every second breath you take comes from photosynthesis — the oxygen released by plants, algae, and cyanobacteria over billions of years built our atmosphere.
</Callout>
The glucose produced feeds nearly every food chain, and the process also removes CO₂ from the atmosphere, helping regulate climate.

<Card title="Try It Yourself">
Want to see photosynthesis in action? Place a sprig of Elodea (an aquatic plant) in a test tube of water with baking soda (CO₂ source), put it in bright light, and watch for bubbles of oxygen rising from the leaves. Count bubbles per minute to measure the rate.
</Card>
