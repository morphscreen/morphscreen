# The Planets of Our Solar System

Our solar system is home to eight planets, each with its own unique character, from scorching rocky worlds to icy giants. They all orbit the Sun, held by its gravity, and are split into two main groups: the inner rocky planets and the outer gas and ice giants.

<Card title="The Inner Rocky Planets" subtitle="Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars">
These four worlds are small, dense, and made mostly of rock and metal. They have solid surfaces, few or no moons, and are much closer to the Sun than the giants beyond.

<Tabs>
<Tab title="Mercury">
Mercury is the smallest planet and the closest to the Sun. Its surface is heavily cratered, like our Moon, and it has almost no atmosphere. Temperatures swing wildly: from 430°C in the day to -180°C at night.

<Tab title="Venus">
Venus is the hottest planet, wrapped in a thick, toxic atmosphere of carbon dioxide with clouds of sulfuric acid. A runaway greenhouse effect traps heat, making its surface hot enough to melt lead. It spins backwards, so the Sun rises in the west.

<Tab title="Earth">
Earth is the only world we know that hosts life. About 71% of its surface is covered with liquid water, and its oxygen-rich atmosphere protects us from solar radiation. It has one large moon, which stabilizes its tilt and drives the tides.

<KeyValue title="Earth facts">

<Tab title="Mars">
Mars is the "Red Planet," colored by iron oxide (rust) on its surface. It has the tallest volcano in the solar system (Olympus Mons, 21 km high) and a canyon system (Valles Marineris) that would stretch across the US. It once had rivers and lakes, and may have hosted microbial life.

<Card title="The Outer Giant Planets" subtitle="Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune">
Beyond the asteroid belt lie the giants. Jupiter and Saturn are gas giants, mostly hydrogen and helium. Uranus and Neptune are ice giants, with more water, ammonia, and methane. All four have rings and many moons.

<Tabs>
<Tab title="Jupiter">
Jupiter is the largest planet, more than twice as massive as all the others combined. Its Great Red Spot is a storm larger than Earth that has raged for centuries. It has at least 95 moons, including Ganymede, the largest moon in the solar system.

<KeyValue title="Jupiter facts">

<Tab title="Saturn">
Saturn is famous for its spectacular ring system, made of billions of ice and rock particles. It is the least dense planet — it would float in water. Its moon Titan has a thick atmosphere and lakes of liquid methane.

<Tab title="Uranus">
Uranus is tilted on its side, likely from a massive ancient collision, so it rolls around the Sun. Its blue-green color comes from methane in its atmosphere. It is the coldest planet, with an average temperature of about -224°C.

<Tab title="Neptune">
Neptune is the farthest planet, a deep blue world with the fastest winds in the solar system, reaching 2,100 km/h. It was the first planet found by mathematical prediction rather than direct observation. Its moon Triton orbits backwards and has geysers of nitrogen.

<Card title="A Brief History of Discovery">
<Steps>
<Step title="Ancient times" status="done" subtitle="Mercury to Saturn visible to the naked eye">
The five planets closest to the Sun (excluding Earth) were known to ancient astronomers in Babylon, Greece, China, and elsewhere. They were called "wanderers" because they moved against the fixed stars.
</Step>
<Step title="1781" status="done" subtitle="Uranus found by William Herschel">
Herschel, a British astronomer, discovered Uranus with a telescope, doubling the known size of the solar system. It was the first planet found in modern times.
</Step>
<Step title="1846" status="done" subtitle="Neptune predicted by math">
Urbain Le Verrier and John Couch Adams independently calculated Neptune's position from wobbles in Uranus's orbit. Johann Galle found it the same night he looked.
</Step>
<Step title="2006" status="done" subtitle="Pluto reclassified as a dwarf planet">
The International Astronomical Union defined what a planet is, and Pluto — along with Eris and Ceres — was reclassified as a dwarf planet, leaving eight planets.
</Step>
</Steps>
</Card>

<Card title="Beyond the Eight">
Not every world orbiting the Sun is a planet. Dwarf planets like Pluto, Ceres, and Eris are large enough to be round but haven't cleared their orbits of other debris. There are also countless asteroids in the belt between Mars and Jupiter, and icy objects in the Kuiper Belt beyond Neptune.

<Callout tone="warning" title="Pluto's demotion">
Pluto was discovered in 1930 and long considered the ninth planet. But with the discovery of other similar worlds, astronomers defined three criteria for planethood — Pluto fails the third, so it is now a dwarf planet.
</Callout>
</Card>

<Card title="Fun Facts">
- **Venus is the brightest natural object** in our night sky after the Moon.
- **Saturn's rings are incredibly thin** — about 10 meters thick in places, relative to a 280,000 km width.
- **A year on Neptune is 165 Earth years** — it has only completed one orbit since its discovery in 1846.
- **Mars has seasons** like Earth, because its axis is tilted at a similar angle.
- **Jupiter's Great Red Spot is shrinking** — it was once wide enough to hold three Earths; now it fits just over one.
